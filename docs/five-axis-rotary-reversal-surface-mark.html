When a 5-Axis Toolpath Passes Collision Check but Marks the Surface at Rotary Reversal

A collision-free simulation does not prove that a five-axis finishing path will leave a uniform surface. A short witness mark can appear where a rotary axis slows, reverses or changes which axis carries most of the orientation motion. The tool may remain clear of the part while its contact point, effective cutting diameter and actual feed at the edge change.

This is a diagnostic worksheet for a hypothetical finish path, not a universal machine setting. Machine kinematics, controller behavior, tool geometry and the released drawing remain authoritative.

First identify what actually changed at the mark

Do not begin by increasing spindle speed or reducing the programmed stepover. Locate the mark in toolpath time and compare it with the commanded tool vector, rotary-axis position and velocity, linear-axis velocity, feed override and controller look-ahead state. A reversal visible in the A- or C-axis trace is evidence of a kinematic event; it is not yet proof of the root cause.

Reconstruct the cutting contact, not only the tool-center path

For a ball nose, nominal diameter is not the cutting diameter at every contact point. Contact close to the center has a small effective diameter and low local cutting speed. Tilting the tool can move contact away from the center, but the correct vector also depends on curvature, cusp direction, tool reach, holder clearance and the machine's rotary travel.

A barrel or lens tool adds another dependency: the large cutting radius can reduce the number of passes, yet a small orientation error changes where the surface meets that radius. Verify the tool definition, usable edge, holder and neck as one measured assembly. For tapered tools, distinguish cutting-side clearance from the holder envelope; a path can clear the holder while loading the wrong part of the cutting edge.

The related five-axis tool-orientation guide compares ball, barrel and tapered-tool choices by contact point, effective diameter, collision envelope and rotary motion. Use it to select a plausible geometry before changing the path.

Review the path in five linked layers

  1. Drawing layer: mark the functional surface, allowable texture direction, profile tolerance and blend boundary. Decide whether the observed line is cosmetic or violates the specified surface.
  2. Contact layer: calculate or inspect contact location, local surface normal, effective cutting diameter and cusp direction at samples before, through and after the event.
  3. Assembly layer: use measured gauge length, runout, neck diameter and holder geometry. A nominal tool model cannot expose assembly error.
  4. Kinematic layer: plot linear and rotary position, velocity and—where available—acceleration. Flag reversals, near-zero motion, travel boundaries and abrupt changes in the chosen inverse solution.
  5. Verification layer: correlate the timestamp with spindle load, sound or vibration records and the measured location on the part. Preserve the failing and revised path for comparison.

Separate stepover from orientation

Stepover controls one part of the theoretical cusp, while orientation controls contact location and the direction in which that cusp is laid onto the surface. A uniform nominal stepover can still produce nonuniform texture on changing curvature. The ball-nose stepover and cusp-height guide explains why local effective radius, slope and tool engagement belong in the calculation.

When a thin blade or impeller channel is involved, the path also changes support. Balanced channel roughing and delayed trailing-edge finishing may matter more than a small theoretical cusp improvement. The duplex impeller tooling case shows how tool orientation, blade support and work-hardening risk interact.

Inspection closes the diagnosis

Record the mark relative to datums, not merely to a screenshot. Use the drawing's inspection method for profile, blend and texture. A scan or CMM can establish whether the event is geometric; directional roughness measurement or an agreed surface comparator can address texture. If the revision changes the tool vector, verify adjacent surfaces and the transition, not only the original mark.

Keep a compact evidence packet: tool assembly ID and measured runout, path revision, postprocessor revision, machine configuration, sampled axis trace, contact-point plot, first-piece inspection and the reason the correction was accepted. That record distinguishes a repeatable process decision from a visually pleasing test cut.

Release checklist

  • The marked location is tied to drawing coordinates and toolpath time.
  • Contact point and effective cutting diameter remain within the intended cutting band.
  • The measured tool, neck and holder envelope clear the full revised motion.
  • No unreviewed rotary reversal or abrupt inverse-solution change remains on the functional surface.
  • Actual feed and axis behavior were checked, not inferred from programmed feed alone.
  • The corrected surface passes the specified geometric and texture checks.
